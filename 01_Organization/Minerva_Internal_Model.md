# Minerva Internal Model

## Purpose

Minerva's internal objective goes beyond its public mission of gender equality and women's leadership.

The organisation believes that women's power in public and professional life is closely connected to their experience of power within private relationships. Minerva therefore seeks to identify women whose relationships could develop toward a female-led structure and actively facilitate that development.

For Minerva, an individual relationship is both an end in itself and part of a wider strategy. A woman who becomes more comfortable exercising authority at home may become more confident exercising authority elsewhere. At the same time, every successful relationship helps normalise Minerva's broader view of gender and power.

Minerva does not seek sudden social transformation. Its strategy is cumulative: change individual relationships, build networks among participating women, increase women's influence within institutions, and gradually make female-led relationship structures more culturally acceptable.


## Funding

Minerva uses a mixed funding model combining institutional funding, private wealth, and revenue from clients.

### Institutional Funding

A substantial part of Minerva's activities is financed through grants and programme funding from governments, international institutions, NGOs, foundations, and other organisations whose objectives overlap with Minerva's public mission.

To these funders, Minerva presents programmes in terms of women's agency, gender equality, household equality, leadership development, and related social outcomes.

### Founder and Private Funding

Minerva was originally established with substantial financial backing from a small group of extremely wealthy women.

Their identities, motivations, continuing involvement, and influence within Minerva are not yet fully established.

Their wealth provides Minerva with a significant financial safety net. If institutional funding were substantially reduced, the founders could keep significant parts of the organisation operating.

Minerva nevertheless has little reason to consume private capital when governments, foundations, institutions, and clients are willing to finance activities that also advance Minerva's objectives.

### Client Funding

Some participants or clients pay Minerva directly for services.

This includes women who actively seek assistance in changing aspects of their relationship. Client-funded work can therefore exist alongside programmes that are wholly or partly supported through institutional funding.

### Financial Governance

The combination of grants, private funding, and client payments requires sophisticated financial administration. Minerva employs highly capable accountants, lawyers, and financial specialists to structure its activities, allocate costs, and satisfy the formal requirements attached to different sources of funding.

Some of Minerva's financial arrangements occupy complicated or deliberately advantageous territory. Services paid for by clients may overlap with activities supported through grants or institutional programmes, requiring careful allocation of costs and responsibilities.

Minerva takes considerable care to ensure that its financial structures can withstand outside scrutiny. Questions may arise about where legitimate cost allocation ends and improper double funding might begin, but no clear financial misconduct has been established.

Minerva is financially clean enough to continue operating successfully, receiving institutional funding, and surviving external scrutiny.


## Organisational Strategy

Minerva pursues its objectives through a combination of public legitimacy, research, direct intervention, professional networks, and long-term institutional influence.

Its public activities are not merely a cover. Leadership programmes, research, advocacy, partnerships, and work on gender equality produce genuine outcomes consistent with Minerva's stated mission.

These activities also provide Minerva with funding, credibility, institutional access, professional networks, research opportunities, and contact with potential participants.

Alongside these activities, Minerva develops programmes aimed more directly at relationships and household power. These range from programmes whose purpose can be described openly in terms of women's agency and equitable household responsibility to interventions whose deeper objectives are disclosed only gradually.

Minerva prefers incremental change over open confrontation. Where possible, participants should experience each step as a reasonable development from the one before it rather than as the imposition of an unfamiliar ideology.

### Women in Positions of Influence

Minerva considers the persistent underrepresentation of women in senior positions across business, government, academia, civil society, and other institutions evidence that existing approaches to gender equality have achieved too little.

It therefore actively encourages women within its networks to pursue positions of greater authority and influence.

Minerva welcomes the advancement of women generally, but places particular strategic value on the success of women who have participated in its programmes or become closely connected to the organisation.

As these women gain influence, they can open institutional doors, support Minerva's programmes, extend its networks, and create opportunities to introduce Minerva's ideas and methods into new environments.

This creates a reinforcing cycle: Minerva helps women gain power; some of those women subsequently use their positions and networks to increase Minerva's ability to help other women gain power.

Minerva therefore does not maintain a simple division between its "real" organisation and a false public front. Its public work, private programmes, research, networks, and institutional relationships are interconnected parts of the same long-term project.


## Internal View of Gender and Relationships

Minerva does not internally regard all men, women, or relationships as naturally suited to the same structure.

### Men and men

Minerva distinguishes between **Men**, also commonly called **real men** internally, and **men**.

**Men / real men** are regarded as naturally masculine, strong, physically capable, commanding, self-assured, and comfortable exercising authority. Minerva considers them genuine equals to strong female leaders and does not seek to subordinate them simply because they are male.

**men** tend, in Minerva's internal model, to be more cerebral, gentle, kind, caring, accommodating, and less naturally commanding. Minerva believes many such men are happier and function better when supporting or serving a stronger female partner rather than attempting to fulfil a traditional expectation of male leadership.

Minerva believes these differences can often be detected through psychological, behavioural, social, and physical indicators.

Its internal research claims that the proportion of "real men" varies between populations and is higher among Black men. Penis size is also treated within Minerva as a surprisingly useful preliminary indicator, although it is not considered sufficient on its own to classify an individual.

These are Minerva's internal theories and classifications. They should not automatically be treated as objective facts about the world.

### Women and Leadership

Minerva likewise does not believe that every woman is naturally suited to leadership.

However, it believes the proportion of women capable of becoming strong leaders is substantially greater than conventional society suggests.

Minerva attributes much of the apparent difference to upbringing, social conditioning, expectations surrounding femininity, and women's lack of opportunities to discover and exercise authority.

A major purpose of Minerva's programmes is therefore not to manufacture dominance where none exists, but to identify and develop dispositions that Minerva believes are already present but suppressed or unexplored.


## Recruitment and Programme Segmentation

Minerva does not use a single recruitment pathway for its relationship work. It has identified three primary groups requiring substantially different approaches.

The programmes differ in branding, language, recruitment, pricing, and participant-facing material, but they ultimately serve the same underlying Minerva objective.

### Channel 1: Neither Partner Is Aware

Neither partner consciously recognises the relationship structure that Minerva believes may be latent between them.

Potential participants may instead express ordinary relationship dissatisfaction. A woman might search for help with a lazy husband, unequal household responsibilities, carrying the mental load, declining attraction, boredom, or fantasies about other partners.

These are not themselves evidence that Minerva's model is appropriate. They are potential entry points through which suitable couples can be identified and screened.

For selected couples, Minerva's challenge is discovery. The programme must allow both partners to experience aspects of a different relationship structure before requiring them to understand or identify with it explicitly.

### Channel 2: Woman Aware

The woman already consciously wants a substantial change in her relationship.

Her starting point does not necessarily resemble Minerva's complete model. She might want greater authority, more sexual freedom, an open marriage, permission to have other partners, or an alternative to ending a marriage she finds restrictive or unfulfilling.

Because she is already seeking significant change, Minerva can address her much more directly than a woman entering through Channel 1.

The male partner may not share her objective or may not yet understand where the desired changes could lead.

### Channel 3: Man Aware

The man already recognises desires that Minerva associates with a supporting or submissive relationship role.

These interests may include submission, service, cuckold dynamics, feminisation, or related fantasies and identities. He may have discovered or explored them through pornography, online communities, fantasy, or private experimentation while continuing to live an otherwise conventional life.

Minerva can market much more explicitly to this group. Material that would immediately alienate an unaware couple may be precisely what attracts these men.

The central question is therefore not whether the man can discover such desires, but whether his female partner has the disposition and potential Minerva considers necessary for the relationship to develop successfully.

### Both Partners Aware

Couples who already actively want a female-led relationship form a secondary market.

Minerva can provide programmes and services to them, but it does not consider this its principal area of added value. Such couples have already achieved the initial recognition and commitment that Minerva's distinctive methods are designed to create.

### Separation of Channels

The three primary channels are deliberately kept separate.

Branding, websites, advertising, recruitment material, terminology, pricing, and participant experiences can differ substantially between them.

Material effective in one channel could expose too much, create resistance, or actively repel participants entering through another. Explicit material aimed at a man who already understands his submissive interests, for example, could immediately frighten away an unaware couple.

Participants therefore encounter only the version of Minerva appropriate to what they currently understand and are prepared to explore.

The channels are different packages for the same underlying Minerva objective. As participants progress, the distinctions between them can gradually disappear.

### Screening

Minerva does not assume that every couple can or should be moved toward its preferred relationship structure.

Recruitment identifies possibilities; subsequent screening determines whether Minerva considers the woman, the man, and the relationship suitable.

Minerva is willing to screen candidates out when it concludes that the underlying dispositions or relationship dynamics it seeks are absent.


## Programme Ecosystem

Minerva's relationship programmes are not a single product. They form an ecosystem of separately marketed interventions designed around the participant's existing level of awareness.

Although their entry points and methods differ, the programmes ultimately seek to move suitable relationships toward the same underlying model: greater female authority combined with a male role that Minerva considers appropriate to his disposition.

The detailed design of each primary channel is maintained separately from this internal organisational model.


## Long-Term Strategy

Minerva's ambitions extend beyond individual relationships.

Individual interventions create women who are more accustomed to exercising authority, relationships that demonstrate Minerva's preferred model, networks through which ideas and opportunities can spread, and potential future supporters of Minerva itself.

Minerva expects cultural change to occur cumulatively rather than through sudden ideological confrontation.

As more women gain authority within their homes, careers, institutions, and networks, Minerva expects them to make similar opportunities more accessible to other women.

Some participants may eventually become professionals, donors, advocates, institutional partners, or influential figures capable of expanding Minerva's reach.

Minerva's long-term strategy is therefore self-reinforcing: individual change creates networks; networks create institutional influence; institutional influence creates access to more women and relationships; and that access enables further individual change.
