# Minerva Core Theory

## How to Use This File

This document describes the central theories through which Minerva interprets gender, relationships, domestic life, sexuality, professional success, and social change.

These theories are sincerely believed within Minerva. They guide Minerva's research priorities, programme design, participant selection, interventions, and long-term strategy.

They are not automatically objective facts of the fictional world.

Minerva conducts real research and does not generally fabricate data. However, its research culture contains strong confirmation bias. Findings consistent with Minerva's theories tend to be accepted readily, while contradictory findings receive greater scrutiny for confounding variables, classification errors, social conditioning, measurement problems, or exceptional circumstances.

Other Minerva documents should be interpreted in light of these theories.

Detailed programme methods belong in the relevant programme documents. Detailed studies, experiments, statistics, and research publications can later be developed separately.


# Central View

Minerva does not believe that gender is irrelevant.

It believes biological sex, personality, sexuality, culture, upbringing, and social expectations all influence human behaviour. Male and female leadership are not assumed to be identical, nor are male and female caregiving.

Minerva's objection is to the assumption that sex determines relationship role.

Its central distinction is between two broad functions:

- leadership, direction, ambition, external focus, and authority;
- caregiving, support, stability, attentiveness, and domestic responsibility.

Minerva believes traditional societies recognised a pattern that was common enough to be useful: dominant and externally focused men were often supported by more caregiving and domestically focused women.

The historical mistake was turning this statistical tendency into a universal rule.

Men were expected to lead because many men fit that role. Women were expected to support because many women fit that role. Over time, society stopped asking which individual actually suited which position.

Minerva therefore does not seek to abolish gender roles. It seeks to separate relationship function from automatic assignment by sex.


# Pillar 1: Domestic Career Support

## Core Claim

Minerva believes exceptional professional careers are strongly affected by the domestic environment supporting them.

Historically, many highly successful men benefited from households in which their career was structurally primary.

Their wives did not merely perform household chores. They assumed responsibility for the household as a functioning system.

This included:

- childcare;
- appointments;
- meals;
- family administration;
- social obligations;
- household maintenance;
- planning;
- remembering;
- anticipating future needs;
- dealing with emergencies;
- noticing problems before they became urgent;
- coordinating the work of other people;
- adapting family life around the career-primary partner.

The result was a form of domestic insulation.

The career-primary man could stay late, travel, relocate, attend professional events, concentrate for long periods, accept difficult assignments, recover after demanding work, and pursue opportunities without continuously calculating what would happen at home.

Domestic life functioned as infrastructure behind his career.

## Tasks Versus Responsibility

Minerva distinguishes strongly between performing household tasks and carrying responsibility for the household.

A husband may cook, clean, collect children from school, and perform a substantial amount of domestic work while his wife remains the person who:

- notices what needs doing;
- decides when it must happen;
- remembers deadlines;
- assigns or requests tasks;
- anticipates future problems;
- keeps track of whether everything has actually been completed.

Minerva describes this as the household executive burden.

In its view, transferring individual chores without transferring responsibility does not recreate the support historically available to many career-focused men.

## Why the Shared Household Is Insufficient

Minerva considers the modern shared household an improvement in fairness but not an optimal structure for producing exceptional careers.

If both partners remain equally responsible for domestic life:

- unexpected problems still require negotiation;
- travel or late work requires consultation;
- career opportunities must be balanced against the other partner's needs;
- someone must still coordinate shared responsibilities;
- neither career is completely insulated from domestic disruption.

Minerva therefore argues that equal division of labour and career optimisation are different objectives.

A 50/50 household may be fair, but it does not give either partner the traditional career advantage created when another person takes responsibility for protecting their time and attention.

## Cultural Preference for the Male Career

Minerva believes formal equality has not removed inherited assumptions about whose career matters more.

Even couples who consciously believe both careers are equally important may subconsciously treat the man's career as:

- more serious;
- more economically important;
- more permanent;
- more deserving of sacrifice;
- more central to household identity.

Existing wage differences can reinforce this pattern.

If the man already earns more, protecting his career appears economically rational.

Protecting his career then gives him greater opportunity to:

- work longer;
- travel;
- accept promotions;
- build networks;
- take professional risks;
- accumulate experience.

His earnings increase further.

The larger earnings gap then becomes additional evidence that his career should remain the priority.

Minerva sees this as a self-reinforcing cycle rather than necessarily conscious discrimination.

## The Minerva Reversal

Minerva's solution is not merely to divide domestic work more equally.

It seeks, in suitable relationships, to reverse the traditional career-support structure.

The woman's career becomes structurally primary.

Her partner increasingly assumes responsibility for:

- running the household;
- anticipating domestic needs;
- managing logistics;
- absorbing routine disruption;
- dealing with emergencies;
- organising family responsibilities;
- adapting domestic life around her professional requirements.

The woman should increasingly be able to treat domestic problems as matters that will be handled rather than matters she must personally manage.

Minerva predicts that placing women in the historically advantaged position of the career-primary partner will substantially increase their professional advancement.

## Compounding Advantage

Minerva believes relatively small differences accumulate over long periods.

A woman does not need to be explicitly prevented from becoming successful.

It may be enough that she:

- declines slightly more travel;
- misses some networking opportunities;
- chooses more predictable positions;
- comes home earlier;
- takes fewer professional risks;
- turns down one difficult assignment;
- accepts more interruptions.

Repeated over ten or twenty years, Minerva believes these differences can create large gaps in seniority, income, influence, and professional confidence.


# Pillar 2: Role Alignment and Sexual Agency

## Role Alignment Theory

Minerva believes relationships function best when leadership and caregiving roles align with the underlying dispositions of the partners rather than being automatically assigned according to sex.

Many men fit traditional masculine leadership roles.

Many women fit traditional caregiving roles.

Minerva does not consider either arrangement inherently problematic.

Its concern is the substantial minority for whom the traditional assignment is reversed.

Some women are naturally more suited to:

- leadership;
- decision-making;
- ambition;
- authority;
- external competition;
- risk-taking.

Some men are naturally more suited to:

- care;
- support;
- attentiveness;
- emotional responsiveness;
- stability;
- service;
- domestic management.

Minerva believes traditional gender systems systematically mishandle these pairings.

## Men and Real Men

Minerva's internal model distinguishes between men who are naturally suited to caregiving and support and the more traditionally dominant men it often calls `Men` or `real men`.

Detailed classification criteria are defined elsewhere in Minerva's internal documentation.

For the purpose of this theory, the distinction is functional.

A caregiving man can provide:

- stability;
- devotion;
- attentiveness;
- emotional security;
- practical support;
- reliable domestic management.

A Real Man can provide a very different interpersonal and sexual experience associated with:

- confidence;
- physical presence;
- assertiveness;
- competitiveness;
- independence;
- masculine status;
- sexual confidence.

Minerva does not believe these two roles necessarily need to be performed by the same male partner.

## Sexual Agency

Minerva believes the dominant partner benefits from meaningful sexual autonomy.

The theory is not simply that outside sex should be tolerated.

Minerva argues that choosing partners, being desired, pursuing attraction, exercising sexual freedom, and successfully crossing inherited boundaries can reinforce a woman's sense of:

- agency;
- desirability;
- status;
- confidence;
- entitlement to make choices;
- comfort exercising power;
- willingness to take risks.

Minerva considers this particularly significant for women because it believes women are often socialised toward sexual restraint, accommodation, loyalty, modesty, and concern about judgement.

A woman who successfully acts outside those expectations may therefore experience more than sexual novelty.

Minerva interprets the experience as evidence to herself that inherited rules do not necessarily govern her choices.

## Two Forms of Intimacy

Minerva does not assume that sexual intimacy with the caregiving husband disappears.

The husband may remain an important romantic and sexual partner.

Intimacy with him may centre on:

- attachment;
- tenderness;
- familiarity;
- emotional safety;
- affection;
- trust;
- devotion;
- service;
- her pleasure.

Sexual access remains ultimately at the woman's discretion within Minerva's preferred relationship structure.

Minerva distinguishes this from sexual experiences with more dominant outside partners.

These experiences may be associated with:

- novelty;
- challenge;
- excitement;
- status;
- physical confidence;
- risk;
- strong sexual activation;
- the experience of being desired by confident and socially powerful men.

Minerva therefore treats the two forms of intimacy as potentially complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

## Security at Home, Activation Outside

A common Minerva formulation is:

**security at home combined with activation outside.**

The caregiving husband provides the secure base.

He provides emotional attachment, devotion, domestic stability, practical support, and freedom from household responsibility.

Outside partners can provide novelty, challenge, sexual status, and experiences that Minerva believes reinforce assertiveness and self-confidence.

Minerva considers this combination particularly powerful for women whose traditional upbringing previously connected female sexuality with restraint, dependence, or male ownership.

## Biological and Behavioural Research

Minerva does not rely on a claim that sexual experiences biologically transform women through a single mechanism.

Its researchers instead study modest physiological and behavioural effects.

Possible measurements include:

- cortisol;
- salivary testosterone;
- stress responses;
- heart-rate variability;
- self-reported confidence;
- assertiveness;
- risk tolerance;
- negotiation behaviour;
- willingness to pursue difficult goals;
- willingness to seek promotion or professional responsibility.

Minerva is particularly interested in whether women display measurable short-term increases in confidence, assertiveness, competitive behaviour, or willingness to take risks following successful experiences of sexual agency.

Biological effects are generally treated as supporting evidence rather than proof of dramatic biological change.

Minerva may nevertheless interpret relatively small effects enthusiastically when they fit its larger theory.


# Pillar 3: Role Liberation and Male Relief

## Traditional Masculinity as Burden

Minerva believes the traditional gender system harms a significant minority of men as well as women.

Men who are naturally caring, gentle, attentive, supportive, or comfortable following rather than leading are nevertheless expected to conform to conventional masculine expectations.

They may be expected to be:

- dominant;
- decisive;
- competitive;
- career-driven;
- sexually assertive;
- independent;
- emotionally contained;
- physically confident;
- responsible for leadership.

Minerva believes that men whose underlying dispositions do not fit this model may spend much of their lives attempting to perform an identity that feels unnatural.

## Psychological Cost

Minerva associates this mismatch with:

- anxiety;
- chronic performance pressure;
- shame;
- fear of inadequacy;
- fear of social judgement;
- difficulty expressing vulnerability;
- uncertainty about what masculinity requires;
- suppressed desires;
- dissatisfaction within relationships.

Such men may appear conventionally masculine because they have learned that failure to perform masculinity carries social consequences.

Minerva therefore does not assume outward presentation reliably reveals underlying disposition.

## The Caregiving Alternative

For suitable men, Minerva presents a caregiving role not as a reduction of status but as liberation from an unsuitable role.

The man no longer needs to prove that he is:

- the strongest;
- the most ambitious;
- the final decision-maker;
- sexually dominant;
- constantly independent;
- responsible for directing the relationship.

Instead, he can build identity around:

- devotion;
- reliability;
- service;
- care;
- attentiveness;
- emotional connection;
- domestic competence;
- supporting a person he loves.

Minerva believes such men may experience substantial relief when relationship expectations become clear.

Rather than continuously deciding how masculinity requires them to behave, they can orient themselves around one primary relational source of direction.

## Joy in Service

Minerva does not want caregiving men merely to tolerate service.

Its programmes aim to help them discover pride and satisfaction in being excellent at it.

Service can become:

- an expression of love;
- a source of competence;
- a stable identity;
- a form of intimacy;
- a source of praise and recognition;
- a way to contribute meaningfully to the success of the household and partner.

Minerva therefore treats caregiving as skilled work rather than passive submission.

## Sexual Service Rather Than Sexual Performance

Minerva extends the same principle into intimacy.

Traditional masculinity can place pressure on men to perform sexually through:

- conquest;
- penetration;
- endurance;
- dominance;
- initiating;
- proving desirability;
- satisfying conventional expectations of male sexuality.

Minerva teaches suitable men that sexual value can instead come from:

- attentiveness;
- responsiveness;
- patience;
- service;
- prioritising their partner's pleasure;
- emotional intimacy;
- devotion.

This allows intimacy to become another field in which the man stops performing an imposed identity and begins expressing the role Minerva believes better suits him.

## Stronger Social Enforcement

Minerva believes male role conditioning can sometimes be more difficult to undo than female conditioning.

A woman who departs from conventional femininity may encounter resistance, but men who visibly depart from conventional masculinity can face particularly strong social enforcement from other men and from society more broadly.

Possible pressures include:

- ridicule;
- exclusion;
- accusations of weakness;
- sexual humiliation;
- loss of male social status;
- fear of rejection;
- fear of being seen as insufficiently masculine.

Minerva believes these pressures create a strong incentive for men to bury caregiving or submissive tendencies even from themselves.

A man may therefore require substantial psychological safety before he can genuinely explore whether the conventional role ever suited him.


# Pillar 4: Suppressed Disposition and Social Conditioning

## Latent Traits

Minerva believes many people do not know their own preferred role because they have never been permitted to explore alternatives.

A woman may sincerely believe she is not dominant because she has spent her entire life being rewarded for accommodation.

A man may sincerely believe he must lead because he has never experienced another identity as socially legitimate.

Minerva therefore distinguishes between:

- current behaviour;
- socially performed behaviour;
- underlying disposition.

This distinction is fundamental to its participant selection and programme design.

## Female Leadership Potential

Minerva believes latent female leadership is substantially more common than visible female dominance suggests.

Girls and women may learn to:

- soften commands;
- apologise for ambition;
- avoid appearing controlling;
- prioritise harmony;
- defer to male confidence;
- reduce visible competitiveness;
- associate female leadership with selfishness or aggression.

Minerva therefore does not require women to arrive already confident, dominant, or consciously interested in authority.

It looks for potential as well as existing behaviour.

## Suppressed Male Caregiving

Minerva applies the same logic to men.

A man may have learned to suppress:

- desire to please;
- preference for supporting roles;
- tenderness;
- emotional dependence;
- enjoyment of service;
- comfort following a stronger partner.

Minerva believes these tendencies can remain hidden beneath years of masculine performance.

## Identity Follows Experience

Minerva does not believe argument alone is sufficient to overcome lifelong conditioning.

This is one of the central justifications for its experiential programmes.

People often understand themselves differently only after experiencing themselves behaving differently.

A woman may intellectually accept that she could lead while still feeling uncomfortable doing so.

The first time she:

- gives an instruction and it is followed;
- makes an important decision without asking permission;
- prioritises her career;
- exercises sexual choice;
- expects another person to adapt to her needs;

she receives experiential evidence about herself that abstract education cannot provide.

The same applies to men.

A man may discover that:

- following does not make him unsafe;
- serving can produce pride;
- caregiving can earn respect;
- surrendering responsibility can feel relieving;
- prioritising his partner can provide stronger intimacy than performing masculinity.

Minerva therefore deliberately creates opportunities for participants to experience alternative roles before demanding that they define themselves using those roles.

Repeated behaviour can gradually become identity.

## Cultural Resistance as an Active Barrier

Minerva sees upbringing, shame, social expectation, traditional relationship models, peer pressure, and fear of judgement as active forces preserving misaligned relationships.

It does not consider passive education sufficient to overcome these forces.

If decades of conditioning created the existing behaviour, Minerva believes meaningful counter-experience is required to loosen it.

This belief provides Minerva with much of the moral justification for its more interventionist programme design.


# Supporting Principle: Agency Transfer

Minerva believes authority learned in one sphere can influence behaviour in another.

A woman who repeatedly practises:

- making decisions;
- giving direction;
- negotiating;
- setting expectations;
- accepting service;
- expecting others to accommodate her priorities;

may become more comfortable exercising authority professionally as well.

Minerva does not treat this as the sole explanation for professional advancement, but considers it an additional mechanism connecting private and public power.


# Supporting Principle: Institutional Reproduction

Minerva believes institutions tend to reproduce their existing leadership.

People often mentor, trust, promote, and sponsor people who resemble themselves or fit familiar models of leadership.

Male-dominated leadership structures can therefore reproduce male leadership without requiring deliberate conspiracy.

Increasing the number of women in middle and senior positions changes:

- who is recognised as leadership material;
- who receives mentorship;
- who receives sponsorship;
- whose behaviour is considered normal;
- which professional networks become available.

This principle primarily supports Minerva's professional leadership programmes but also reinforces its belief that individual female advancement has wider strategic value.


# Research Culture and Confirmation Bias

Minerva conducts genuine research and employs highly capable researchers.

Its weakness is not generally direct falsification.

Instead, Minerva's institutional culture strongly rewards findings consistent with its existing theories.

Researchers know what Minerva believes the correct underlying model to be.

A supportive result is likely to be interpreted as another piece of evidence.

A contradictory result may provoke questions about:

- methodology;
- participant classification;
- social conditioning;
- hidden relationship dynamics;
- confounding variables;
- external support systems;
- measurement quality;
- insufficient follow-up time.

This produces asymmetric scrutiny.

Correcting a result toward Minerva's established model may be regarded as scientific diligence.

Correcting a result away from the model may require unusually strong evidence.

No explicit instruction to manipulate data is necessary.

## Self-Reinforcing Role Alignment Research

Role Alignment Theory is particularly vulnerable to becoming self-confirming.

If two highly career-focused partners struggle to maintain domestic life, Minerva sees evidence that the household lacks sufficient role specialisation.

If two strongly caregiving partners appear stagnant or professionally unsuccessful, Minerva sees evidence that the relationship lacks a sufficiently dominant partner.

If a relationship apparently contradicts the theory, Minerva may conclude that:

- one partner is secretly or latently dominant;
- one partner is performing a role created by social conditioning;
- apparent equality conceals an actual decision-maker;
- outside wealth or domestic help compensates for misalignment;
- the couple has not been observed long enough.

Minerva therefore possesses a theory that can generate useful predictions while simultaneously becoming increasingly difficult to falsify from inside the organisation.

Every successful intervention becomes evidence that Minerva understood the participants correctly.

Many failures can be interpreted as incorrect classification, insufficient intervention, or outside resistance rather than failure of the theory itself.


# Strategic Implications

Minerva believes these theories require active intervention rather than passive advocacy.

They lead Minerva to:

- transfer meaningful domestic responsibility toward suitable caregiving male partners;
- make the woman's career structurally primary in suitable relationships;
- encourage women to exercise rather than merely discuss authority;
- identify leadership potential hidden by social conditioning;
- identify caregiving tendencies hidden by masculine performance;
- teach men to value care, service, devotion, and domestic competence;
- reduce male sexual performance pressure by reframing intimacy around attentiveness and service;
- encourage substantial sexual autonomy for the dominant partner where Minerva believes it supports confidence and agency;
- distinguish secure domestic intimacy from outside sexual activation;
- challenge inherited assumptions that the male career should automatically receive priority;
- challenge cultural rules that equate masculinity with leadership and femininity with caregiving;
- deliberately expose participants to experiences that contradict deeply learned assumptions;
- use repeated experience to consolidate new identities;
- support women moving into positions of greater professional authority;
- treat resistance caused by upbringing, shame, social expectation, and habit as obstacles that can potentially be overcome rather than immediate evidence that the underlying role is unsuitable.

The precise methods used to pursue these objectives are defined in Minerva's programme and operational documents.
