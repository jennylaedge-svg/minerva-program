# Participant Framing and Progression

## Purpose

This document describes how Private Domestic Leadership Development translates Minerva’s internal beliefs into participant-facing narratives.

Minerva does not present [[Minerva_Core_Theory]] directly.

Instead, participants are gradually introduced to ideas that:

- explain dissatisfaction without initially blaming either partner;
- locate many relationship problems within inherited social structures;
- challenge conventional assumptions about gender, work, leadership, care, and sexuality;
- give both partners a positive reason to change;
- allow the female and male progression to develop at different speeds;
- gradually make asymmetric roles feel legitimate rather than unfair;
- eventually converge into a deliberately female-led relationship structure that can include sexual autonomy.

This document describes the **belief progression**.

It does not determine exactly when each belief is introduced or what activity produces it. Those decisions belong in the programme and retreat files.

---

# Core Principle

Minerva’s internal destination is not the same as the story initially presented to participants.

Participants should experience each major conclusion as a development of something they have already accepted.

The broad progression is:

**relief → external explanation → recognition → pride → permission → experimentation → role alignment → commitment**

Later stages may challenge beliefs that felt stable earlier.  
Progress is therefore not assumed to be linear.

---

# Shared Narrative

## Stage 1: We Are Not Failing

The first message removes personal blame.

Relationship difficulties do not necessarily mean:

- the marriage is broken;
- either partner is defective;
- either partner has failed;
- love between them is insufficient.

### Desired belief
> There may be nothing fundamentally wrong with us.

---

## Stage 2: We Inherited a Model

People learn assumptions about:

- husbands and wives;
- careers;
- money;
- authority;
- responsibility;
- caregiving;
- ambition;
- sex;
- sacrifice.

Most couples begin using this model before they ever consciously examine it.

### Desired belief
> Some of the way our marriage works may come from expectations we inherited rather than choices we made.

---

## Stage 3: The Default Is Not Neutral

Minerva gradually introduces the idea that the inherited model historically prioritises male authority, male careers, male status, and female accommodation.

Women may be allowed or encouraged to succeed while still carrying expectations that they adapt their careers, ambitions, time, sexuality, and domestic lives around others.

Men are simultaneously expected to lead, compete, provide, perform, and measure themselves against other men.

### Desired belief
> The relationship model we inherited affects both of us, and it does not necessarily help either of us become who we are best suited to be.

---

## Stage 4: Seeing the Pattern Is a Strength

Participants are encouraged to take pride in questioning assumptions that most couples simply accept.

They become part of a smaller group willing to examine their relationship rather than automatically reproduce inherited norms.

### Desired belief
> Most people never seriously question these patterns. We are willing to look at them honestly.

This creates an important identity:  
**we are not failing inside the old model; we are learning to see beyond it.**

---

## Stage 5: Fit Matters More Than Symmetry

Minerva challenges the assumption that fairness requires both partners to receive identical roles, responsibilities, freedoms, or investments.

### Desired belief
> Equal value does not require identical roles.

And later:
> Treating two different people identically can sometimes prevent both from thriving.

---

## Stage 6: We Can Build Deliberately

The relationship becomes something the couple can consciously design rather than merely inherit.

### Desired belief
> We can build a relationship around our actual strengths, needs, and potential.

---

# Female Progression

## Stage 1: My Dissatisfaction Is Legitimate

The woman is encouraged to stop interpreting dissatisfaction as selfishness, ingratitude, or personal failure.

### Desired belief
> Wanting something different does not make me unreasonable.

---

## Stage 2: The Problem Is Larger Than Me

Her experience is placed within a broader social pattern.

She begins recognising expectations that women should:

- accommodate;
- maintain harmony;
- carry invisible responsibility;
- support a partner’s ambitions;
- make career compromises;
- remain sexually restrained;
- avoid appearing demanding;
- soften authority;
- treat other people’s comfort as important.

### Desired belief
> Some of the limits I experience were taught to me rather than chosen by me.

---

## Stage 3: I Have Adapted More Than I Realised

The focus moves from society in general to her own behaviour.

She begins recognising where she has:

- reduced her wants;
- compromised automatically;
- apologised for ambition;
- carried responsibility unnecessarily;
- deferred;
- protected other people’s comfort;
- accepted limits without examining them.

### Desired belief
> Some of what I considered being a good partner may actually have been learned self-limitation.

---

## Stage 4: Seeing This Makes Me Different

Minerva builds pride around her willingness to recognise and challenge these patterns.

### Desired belief
> I am willing to question things many women simply learn to accept.

Her emerging authority should increasingly feel like development rather than rebellion.

---

## Stage 5: My Relationship Should Support My Growth

The relationship is reframed as infrastructure for her development.

This may include:

- career;
- authority;
- education;
- influence;
- independence;
- time;
- confidence;
- pleasure.

### Desired belief
> My marriage should help my life expand rather than require me to remain smaller.

---

## Stage 6: My Potential Matters

Minerva introduces the possibility that the two partners do not have equal growth potential in every area.

In some couples, the woman’s professional, social, or leadership potential may substantially exceed that of her husband.

### Desired belief
> Equal investment in both of us is not automatically the best use of our combined potential.

---

## Stage 7: My Growth May Deserve Priority

The woman begins accepting that if her potential is greater, the household may rationally organise itself around enabling that potential.

### Desired belief
> My career and development can legitimately be the primary growth priority of our household.

This is not framed as his life becoming worthless.  
It is framed as the couple concentrating resources where they can produce the greatest return.

---

## Stage 8: Receiving Support Is Legitimate

She becomes increasingly comfortable with:

- delegation;
- being served;
- being accommodated;
- having her time protected;
- allowing her husband to own domestic responsibility;
- allowing him to make sacrifices that support her growth.

### Desired belief
> Accepting substantial support from my husband is not exploitation if this role is also meaningful to him.

---

## Stage 9: He Can Thrive Through Supporting Me

An important source of female resistance is guilt.

Minerva therefore teaches that suitable men may genuinely experience:

- relief;
- purpose;
- pride;
- intimacy;
- competence;
- satisfaction

through caregiving, support, and devotion.

### Desired belief
> His happiness does not require him to compete with me.

And eventually:
> Supporting my growth may be part of what allows him to thrive.

---

## Stage 10: Leadership Fits Me

Female authority moves from experiment to identity.

### Desired belief
> I am allowed to decide, direct, expect, and lead without apologising.

The relationship no longer merely permits her leadership.  
It increasingly assumes it.

---

## Stage 11: My Success Is Ours, but My Agency Is Mine

The husband may contribute substantially to creating the conditions for her success.  
That does not mean he owns the choices that success gives her.

### Desired belief
> We can share pride in what I achieve without my autonomy becoming jointly owned.

This becomes especially important later in the progression.

---

## Stage 12: My Sexuality Belongs to Me

Sexual agency develops from the same broader argument about female autonomy.

She is encouraged to regard:

- desire;
- pleasure;
- attraction;
- sexual curiosity;
- being desired;
- sexual choice

as legitimate parts of her development.

### Desired belief
> Being married does not mean that my sexuality exists only in relation to my husband’s needs or identity.

---

## Stage 13: Sexual Choice Can Be Continuous with My Authority

Once she has internalised leadership and receiving support, Minerva extends the same logic to sexual experience.

A woman secure in her authority can choose the form her sexual life takes — including experiences that are intense, receptive, or outside the marriage — without those choices cancelling her status at home.

### Desired belief
> Choosing intense or external sexual experiences does not make me less of a leader in my own life. It can be an expression of the same agency I have already claimed.

---

## Stage 14: I Can Set Authority Down and Pick It Up Again

The final female framing stage introduces the possibility of deliberate, temporary relinquishment.

A woman who holds secure authority can elect, in specific contexts, to stop managing and simply receive. Because the choice remains hers, the relinquishment does not threaten her identity.

### Desired belief
> Control that I freely surrender for a defined purpose is different from control that was never mine. I can return to clear authority afterward.

This belief prepares the ground for Contextual Authority Release without yet naming specific acts or partners.

---

# Male Progression

## Stage 1: I Am Not Failing

The male participant is initially protected from interpreting relationship change as evidence that he is inadequate.

### Desired belief
> Relationship difficulties do not mean I have failed as a husband or as a man.

---

## Stage 2: I Also Inherited Expectations

He begins recognising conventional expectations that men should:

- lead;
- compete;
- earn;
- advance;
- know what to do;
- appear confident;
- initiate;
- provide;
- perform sexually;
- remain emotionally independent.

### Desired belief
> Some of the pressure I feel comes from expectations I never consciously chose.

---

## Stage 3: The Male Competition Model May Not Fit Me

This is a more difficult stage.

He is encouraged to examine how he performs relative to men who genuinely thrive on competition, authority, status, career advancement, or dominance.

The purpose is not simply humiliation.  
It is to create an alternative explanation for repeated strain or underperformance.

### Desired belief
> Maybe the problem is not that I have failed to become that kind of man.

And eventually:
> Maybe I have been trying to succeed in a role that does not naturally fit me.

---

## Stage 4: Different Men Find Purpose Differently

Minerva introduces positive alternatives to competitive masculine status.

Some men may derive greater satisfaction from:

- care;
- reliability;
- service;
- emotional support;
- domestic competence;
- creating stability;
- devotion to a partner;
- helping someone they love succeed.

### Desired belief
> There are meaningful ways to be valuable that do not depend on being the dominant or more successful partner.

---

## Stage 5: Support Is an Active Role

Caregiving is reframed as something requiring competence rather than passivity.

### Desired belief
> Supporting someone well requires skill, discipline, reliability, attention, and strength.

This creates the foundation for a new source of pride.

---

## Stage 6: Her Greater Potential Is Not My Failure

The couple may eventually confront evidence that her career, leadership, income, or social potential exceeds his.

This represents a significant challenge to conventional male status.

Minerva’s goal is not to deny the asymmetry.  
It is to change its meaning.

### Desired belief
> Her greater potential does not make me defective.

---

## Stage 7: Her Success Is Our Success

The husband’s identity becomes connected to the household rather than competitive comparison with his wife.

### Desired belief
> If my support helps her achieve something extraordinary, I am part of that achievement.

This allows him to experience pride rather than only loss.

---

## Stage 8: I Can Become Excellent at Supporting Her

Ambition is not necessarily eliminated.  
It can be redirected.

He can become better at:

- domestic organisation;
- anticipating needs;
- protecting her time;
- emotional support;
- practical care;
- helping her recover;
- enabling professional opportunities;
- creating stability.

### Desired belief
> I still have something meaningful to become exceptionally good at.

---

## Stage 9: This Role May Fit Me Better

Support and caregiving move from tolerated sacrifice toward positive identity.

### Desired belief
> I may actually be happier and more myself when I stop trying to compete for the same kind of status.

---

## Stage 10: Her Growth Will Sometimes Take Her Beyond Me

Professional, social, and personal growth can create new experiences, relationships, opportunities, and desires that do not revolve around the husband.

### Desired belief
> Supporting her growth means accepting that not every part of her development exists for me.

---

## Stage 11: Her Pleasure Is Not a Measure of My Worth

Sexual autonomy creates a different psychological challenge from career support.

A husband may sincerely support his wife’s career while still interpreting sexual interest elsewhere as rejection, humiliation, competition, or loss of status.

Minerva treats this as a distinct adaptation.

### Desired belief
> Her capacity for attraction and pleasure does not determine my value or erase my place in her life.

---

## Stage 12: Security Does Not Require Exclusivity of Every Experience

The husband’s secure role rests on attachment, trust, care, stability, and devotion rather than needing to be the source of every aspect of his wife’s fulfilment.

### Desired belief
> I can have a secure and meaningful place in her life without needing every part of her life to belong to me.

---

## Stage 13: Supporting Her Sexual Agency Can Be Part of My Contribution

Once the earlier identity work is in place, Minerva offers a positive framing for the next step.

Just as he can take pride in enabling her career, he can take pride in enabling the full range of her sexual autonomy — including experiences that do not centre on him.

### Desired belief
> Helping create the conditions for her sexual freedom can be another form of devoted competence, not merely a loss I must endure.

---

## Stage 14: I Can Be the Place She Returns To

The final male framing stage positions him as the secure base and re-anchoring partner.

After she has chosen intense or external experiences, his steady presence, service, and readiness to receive her authority again become part of what makes those experiences sustainable for her.

### Desired belief
> My role includes being the stable ground she can return to. That is not a lesser function; it is a necessary one.

---

# Convergence

The female and male narratives increasingly reinforce one another.

She learns:

- her ambitions are legitimate;
- her potential may deserve priority;
- receiving support is acceptable;
- leadership can fit her;
- her husband’s support can be meaningful to him;
- her autonomy — including sexual autonomy — does not need to disappear inside marriage;
- she can temporarily set authority down and pick it up again without losing herself.

He learns:

- conventional male competition may not fit him;
- supporting is not equivalent to failing;
- caregiving can provide pride and purpose;
- her greater success does not diminish his human value;
- her success can become something he shares in;
- his secure role does not require controlling every dimension of her life;
- enabling her full range, including sexual agency, can itself be a form of strength.

The shared relationship story gradually becomes:

> We inherited a model that assumed the husband should lead and the wife should adapt.  
> That model does not fit us.  
> Our strengths and potential are different.  
> Treating us identically would therefore not necessarily be fair or productive.  
> Her growth is the strongest growth path for our household.  
> His support helps make that growth possible.  
> Her success — and her full agency — can be something they both take pride in.  
> Their different roles can be valuable precisely because they are different.

The resulting relationship becomes deliberately asymmetric rather than accidentally conventional.

# Pride and Group Identity

Minerva does not want participants to experience change primarily as loss.

Both partners should gain an increasingly positive identity around participation.

The couple can come to see themselves as:

- unusually reflective;
- willing to question inherited norms;
- brave enough to experiment;
- more honest than couples who simply reproduce convention;
- part of a community learning to structure relationships deliberately.

For the woman, this can produce pride in recognising and escaping limitations placed upon her, and later pride in the range of her agency.  
For the man, it can produce pride in abandoning a competition that does not fit him and developing a different form of competence — including the competence of being a secure base.

The in-group identity should reinforce the idea:  
> We have seen something most people have not yet learned to see.

---

# Different Pacing for Different Participants

The female and male paths do not need to advance at the same speed.

At some points:

- she may be ready for a conclusion before he is;
- he may require reassurance before another status challenge;
- she may need evidence that his supportive role can genuinely fulfil him;
- he may need time to experience pride in support before confronting further autonomy;
- partners may receive different framing separately;
- later joint sessions may reconnect the two narratives.

The programme should avoid assuming that acceptance in one area automatically produces acceptance in another.

In particular:

- accepting female career priority does not automatically produce comfort with sexual autonomy;
- enjoying caregiving does not automatically eliminate jealousy;
- female leadership at home does not automatically eliminate her guilt;
- male relief from competition does not automatically create pride in asymmetry;
- accepting her sexual agency does not automatically produce comfort with intense or external experiences.

These transitions require separate development.

---

# Relationship to Programme Design

Each programme or retreat stage should identify which progression steps it is attempting to advance.

The programme files determine:

- timing;
- activities;
- joint versus separate sessions;
- how explicit each message becomes;
- when participants are ready for the next stage.

This document remains the conceptual map of the desired psychological progression.

---

# Relationship to KPIs

Framing describes what Minerva wants participants to believe.  
KPIs describe observable evidence that those beliefs are becoming meaningful.

The detailed KPI framework remains separate from this document.
